Airbus backs down on four-day office plan after employee protests
European aircraft manufacturer will retain its existing hybrid work arrangements after a push for more on-site work triggered protests and strikes across several countries

Airbus
 has backed away from plans to require many of its office-based employees to work on site at least four days a week after the proposal triggered an unusually strong reaction from workers across its European operations.
The European aircraft manufacturer had planned to introduce the policy in September, effectively reducing remote work for eligible employees from an average of two days per week to one.

According to 
Reuters
, Airbus has now decided to retain its existing hybrid arrangements, which generally allow eligible employees to work remotely for an average of two days per week.
The retreat follows weeks of protests in France and significantly larger industrial action in Spain, where Airbus employs more than 14,000 people across commercial aircraft, defense and space activities.
CEO Guillaume Faury
 outlined the push for greater office attendance in an internal message sent in June. He argued that Airbus remained fundamentally an industrial company and that having employees working together physically was important for collaboration, creativity and the transfer of knowledge.
The latter has become a particular concern as Airbus has expanded its workforce. According to Reuters, roughly one-third of the company's employees have joined since the COVID-19 pandemic, creating challenges in transferring experience from longer-serving engineers and other specialists to newer staff.

Plan collided with existing agreements
The proposed change quickly encountered another problem: existing labor agreements did not necessarily give Airbus freedom to impose the new policy uniformly.
In France, a 2024 agreement governing quality of working life and remote work remains valid until August 2028. French unions argued that requiring four days of physical attendance would effectively circumvent provisions that had already been negotiated with employees.
The dispute led to demonstrations and short work stoppages at Airbus facilities in France during June.

Resistance became substantially more serious in Spain. Workers began strikes in July over a package of grievances that included salaries, attendance policies and remote working conditions. The action spread across Airbus sites in the country and eventually gained support from several unions.
Around 40% of Airbus' approximately 14,000 Spanish employees participated in the strikes, according to Reuters. Further industrial action had been threatened for September if negotiations failed to produce an agreement.
Guillaume Faury, CEO of Airbus | 
Airbus
Production pressure versus competition for workers
The dispute comes at an awkward time for Airbus. The manufacturer is attempting to increase commercial aircraft production while continuing to struggle with supply-chain constraints and delays affecting several programs.
Airbus is targeting 870 commercial aircraft deliveries in 2026
, making productivity and coordination across engineering, procurement and production particularly sensitive issues.
Faury's argument for greater physical attendance was partly based on those pressures. Airbus maintained that complex aircraft development and manufacturing depend heavily on collaboration between teams and that some activities are more effective when employees work together on site.
Unions countered that reducing flexibility could make Airbus less attractive to engineers, software specialists and other skilled employees who increasingly have employment alternatives outside the traditional aerospace industry.
That issue extends beyond employee satisfaction. Aerospace companies are competing for technical talent not only among themselves but increasingly with technology, artificial intelligence and other industries where hybrid or remote working arrangements can be considerably more flexible.
Airbus has now chosen not to enforce the four-day requirement across the group. Existing arrangements will remain in place, leaving managers and local agreements to determine how frequently eligible employees need to be physically present.
